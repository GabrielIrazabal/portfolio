Rocket avionics bay

Sept 2026 – present · Avionics lead, SSI Rockets · IREC 2027 · Not built yet

I'm the avionics lead on the Stanford Student Space Initiative rocket team. We're building a rocket for the Intercollegiate Rocket Engineering Competition (IREC) in Midland, Texas, in June 2027. This is its avionics bay, or av bay: the electronics section in the middle of the rocket. It sits between the two parachute compartments, with a bulkhead closing off each end, and it holds the computers that track the flight and decide when to deploy the parachutes.

On the pad, we pull arming pins through the side of the rocket, which switches power on to the flight computers. Until then nothing can fire. In flight, two independent computers measure acceleration, rotation and air pressure, which gives altitude. At apogee each one fires an e-match, a small electric igniter that lights a black-powder charge and pushes out the drogue parachute. Lower down, a second charge releases the main. The whole way down, our flight computer radios its GPS position and flight data to the ground, so we can follow the flight live and find the rocket after it lands. A small 4K camera records the flight.

One of the two computers is a commercial altimeter, an Altus Metrum TeleMetrum, because IREC requires a commercial backup. The other is Aerolotl, the team's own flight computer. Each one has its own battery and its own arming switch, so if one computer, battery or switch fails, the other still deploys the parachutes.

There are two custom boards in the bay. I designed the power board, and I helped design the flight computer, Aerolotl, which I also oversee as avionics lead. I modeled the bay itself in Onshape.

Power board

I designed the power board. It holds the four 18650 cells and splits them into three electrically isolated supplies. The TeleMetrum and Aerolotl each get their own cell, pull-pin switch and connector, so a short or a dead cell on one side can't take down the other.

The other two cells, in series, run the camera. A buck converter makes 5 V for the camera and for an ESP32 that starts and stops recording. A divider on the converter's enable pin shuts it off below 5.4 V (about 2.7 V per cell), so the pack never gets over-discharged.

The camera is a RunCam Split 4, which records straight to its own microSD card. The ESP32 runs the camera controller firmware: it talks to the camera over its UART control port, and a phone connects to the ESP32 over Bluetooth to wake the camera and start or stop recording.

Arming is mechanical. Until the pins come out, the flight computers have no power, so no software bug can fire a charge while the rocket is disarmed. I laid the board out around the exact shape of the switch stack, not the other way round, then dropped it into the Onshape bay assembly for a clash check: 0 interferences.

Flight computer

I helped design Aerolotl, which started from the team's earlier Aerolotl rev 2, with the airbrake servo hardware moved onto its own board. As avionics lead I also oversee its development, and it's built to fit the rest of the bay. It takes its power from the power board, and its outline matches the bay CAD, with a notch for the TeleMetrum sled and rubber grommets where the rods pass through.

It's a 4-layer board built around an STM32G473, with motion and pressure sensors, GPS, a LoRa radio for telemetry and a microSD card for logging. The two parachute channels have a hardware arming input in series with their supply, so with it open the igniters can't get power, whatever the firmware does. The firing current is also sized on purpose: two 1 Ω resistors per channel give about 0.86 A from a 3.0 V cell and 1.27 A from a full 4.2 V cell. That stays above the e-match's 0.75 A recommended minimum across the whole battery range, and it still limits the current if an output shorts.

The bay in CAD

Both boards are sized to the inside of the 6 in airframe and stacked on two 3/8"-16 steel threaded rods, with a washer and nut clamping each face. The TeleMetrum stands on a vertical sled bolted to both boards, and the end bulkheads carry the U-bolts for the parachute harnesses and the black-powder charge wells. The model is parametric, with disc size, spacing and rod positions all set as parameters. Both boards come in from KiCad as STEP files, and their outlines, rod holes and cut-outs were drawn to match the CAD exactly.

Where it's at

The boards haven't been built yet, and nothing has flown. Both are designed and checked, and the whole bay fits together in CAD.

High-altitude balloon flight computer

June 2026 – present · Project lead, team of five · Not flown yet

I'm leading a team of five building the avionics for a high-altitude balloon: a main flight computer and a separate APRS board for recovery. We did both boards in KiCad, from schematic capture through layout and DRC, and had JLCPCB fabricate and assemble them.

The main board is 70 × 80 mm, four layers, built around an STM32F411. The IMU, barometer, RTD front end, LoRa radio and SD card all share one SPI bus. GPS is on a UART, and there's a connector for an ArduCAM.

Power comes from a 2S Li-ion pack. The input goes through a P-FET for reverse-polarity protection and a polyfuse, then a TPS63070 buck-boost makes 5 V and an LDO makes 3.3 V. The APRS board takes 5 V over the harness and uses its own TPS63070 to make 4.1 V for the DRA818V.

Both RF paths are 50 Ω microstrip (0.38 mm traces on JLC's JLC04161H stackup) with SMA edge launches and stitched ground vias wherever the RF changes layers.

I wrote the firmware in C. The IMU, barometer, RTD and GPS drivers all work on the real board, and the ground station receives live telemetry and camera images over LoRa. In one bench session the link ran at about −27 dBm RSSI and 11.5 dB SNR, with 0 bad packets out of 902.

Shotbot

Sept 2025 – Feb 2026 · Stanford Moonshot Club, team of five · Electrical system and firmware

A tracked robot with a motorized front arm, built to climb stairs. I designed the electrical system and wrote the ESP32 firmware.

A 3S 5200 mAh LiPo goes through a master switch to a terminal block. Each of the three BTS7960 motor drivers (left track, right track and arm) has its own 20 A fuse, and the logic side has a 2 A fuse feeding an LM2596S buck down to 5 V. The ESP32 sits on a small interconnect PCB and reads an MPU-9250 over I²C. The firmware handles motor control and real-time communication.
