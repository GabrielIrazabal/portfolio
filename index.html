High-altitude balloon flight computer

June 2026 – present · Project lead, team of five · Not flown yet

I'm leading a team of five building the avionics for a high-altitude balloon: a main flight computer and a separate APRS board for recovery. We did both boards in KiCad, from schematic capture through layout and DRC, and had JLCPCB fabricate and assemble them.

The main board is 70 × 80 mm, four layers, built around an STM32F411. The IMU, barometer, RTD front end, LoRa radio and SD card all share one SPI bus. GPS is on a UART, and there's a connector for an ArduCAM.

Power comes from a 2S Li-ion pack. The input goes through a P-FET for reverse-polarity protection and a polyfuse, then a TPS63070 buck-boost makes 5 V and an LDO makes 3.3 V. The APRS board takes 5 V over the harness and uses its own TPS63070 to make 4.1 V for the DRA818V.

Both RF paths are 50 Ω microstrip (0.38 mm traces on JLC's JLC04161H stackup) with SMA edge launches and stitched ground vias wherever the RF changes layers.

I wrote the firmware in C. The IMU, barometer, RTD and GPS drivers all work on the real board, and the ground station receives live telemetry and camera images over LoRa. In one bench session the link ran at about −27 dBm RSSI and 11.5 dB SNR, with 0 bad packets out of 902.

Shotbot

Sept 2025 – Feb 2026 · Stanford Moonshot Club, team of five · Electrical system and firmware

A tracked robot with a motorized front arm, built to climb stairs. I designed the electrical system and wrote the ESP32 firmware.

A 3S 5200 mAh LiPo goes through a master switch to a terminal block. Each of the three BTS7960 motor drivers (left track, right track and arm) has its own 20 A fuse, and the logic side has a 2 A fuse feeding an LM2596S buck down to 5 V. The ESP32 sits on a small interconnect PCB and reads an MPU-9250 over I²C. The firmware handles motor control and real-time communication.

NeuraFlow

TreeHacks 2026 · with Tejasvi Tyagi and Sid Sannabhadti

▶ Demo video (YouTube)

Smart glasses for people with dementia. An ESP32 camera on the frame recognizes people with FaceNet and tells the wearer who they're talking to. A second ESP32 reads heart rate, and if the wearer seems distressed, the assistant steps in. You wake it up by saying "Flow."

ShadowHive

2026 · with Jun and Nathaniel

A wearable kit for teams moving through buildings. An iPhone's LiDAR maps the rooms and an ESP32 draws a mini-map on a wrist screen. UWB radios give each teammate's distance and direction, which works indoors where GPS doesn't. EMG electrodes on the face pick up silently mouthed words, and a model running on an NXP board's NPU classifies them.
